How Long Does It Take to Implement an Overlay Program?

A pension fund's investment committee votes in June to hire an overlay manager. The overlay is supposed to fix the problems the committee has been discussing for two years: cash sitting idle between manager funding dates, a portfolio that drifts off its target allocation every quarter, and a currency exposure nobody has hedged since 2022. The committee asks the obvious question — how long until this is actually running? The overlay manager's answer is the first lesson in how the business works: the trading can start in weeks. The paperwork takes months. And the difference between the two is where most implementation timelines go wrong.

The short answer is that a simple currency overlay can be live in four to eight weeks, while a full multi-strategy overlay program — cash equitization, rebalancing, currency hedging, and completion — typically takes three to six months from mandate to full operation. The range is wide because the work is not one job but four, and the longest of the four is the one the committee never sees.

The four jobs inside the timeline

The first job is the legal and regulatory paperwork, and it is the long pole. Brian Causey, Russell Investments' director of Customized Portfolio Solutions and overlay portfolio management, puts it plainly in twenty years of experience: the preparation that makes an overlay program work — opening new accounts, completing legal and regulatory paperwork, adding over-the-counter trading capabilities — takes days in some cases and several weeks or months in others. An overlay manager that obtains daily custodial information and builds the client's portfolio every day, as Causey describes Russell's mandates, cannot do that work until the custodian agreements, the derivatives documentation, and the counterparty relationships are in place. A pension fund with twenty-three asset managers for its international exposure — one of Russell's documented cases — has twenty-three sets of trading protocols to accommodate, some arriving by SWIFT, some by FTP, some by FIX. The paperwork is not a formality. It is the program.

The second job is the data. The overlay manager needs daily custodial information — every position, every manager, every cash flow — before it can rebalance anything. Russell's FX platform illustrates the scale: its agency-only model runs multi-venue access to more than 25 banks and 50 streaming liquidity providers, and in 2025 it traded over $630 billion in foreign exchange across more than 190 currency pairs. That infrastructure exists because the data work is the program's core. A fund whose custodian feeds arrive late, or whose manager data is inconsistent, will wait for the data long after the contracts are signed. The third job is the trading setup: futures accounts, margin arrangements, and the straight-through processing that lets the overlay manager net and cross orders across managers instead of executing each one separately. In one documented Russell case, a multi-billion-dollar defined-benefit pension fund with 23 asset managers saw its FX execution costs fall because the overlay manager could net orders across managers rather than trading each one in the market. The fourth job is the governance: the investment policy statement amendment, the board approval of the derivatives authority, and the reporting framework that tells the committee what the overlay did and what it cost.

What it costs — and what it saves

The cost of an overlay program is small against the assets it governs, and the public record shows it. Massachusetts' Pension Reserves Investment Management Board — PRIM, one of the largest public pension funds in the United States — budgeted $565,000 for its Parametric overlay program and $900,000 for Russell's foreign-exchange overlay in fiscal 2027, a combined $1.465 million for overlay, FX, and related services. Against a fund of PRIM's size, that is a rounding error. The retail market prices the same service differently: Envestnet's overlay service fee, disclosed in Financial Synergies' March 30, 2026 Form ADV, runs 10 basis points on the first $10 million, 8 basis points on the next $15 million, and 5 basis points above $25 million — a schedule that shows how the fee compresses with scale. Russell's own Form ADV, filed March 31, 2026, reports $81.09 billion of regulatory assets under management as of December 31, 2025, and describes overlay fees as variable with notional exposure plus a base fee for daily analysis and reporting. The structure is the point: the overlay manager charges for the exposure it manages and the daily work it does, not for the assets it holds, because it holds almost none of them.

The savings are easier to measure than the cost. When Russell took over the currency overlay for an Australian superannuation fund that had been using a single currency provider through its custodian's local bank — trading with one counterparty, during Australian hours, when the local currency market was closed — it expanded the counterparties from one to more than ten, and the client achieved over 25% in savings. An independent transaction-cost analysis of Russell's FX trading for January to December 2020 measured savings of 6.305 basis points against the previous program. Those are the numbers that justify the implementation timeline: a program that takes four months to build and saves six basis points a year on a multi-billion-dollar exposure pays for its own delay in weeks.

The mandates that show the scale

The recent mandate flow shows how large the programs have become. In May 2026, Russell won an $8.6 billion foreign-exchange overlay mandate from Income Insurance, one of Singapore's leading composite insurers, across a $33.6 billion multi-asset investment program serving close to 1.4 million customers. In April 2025, France's ERAFP — the pension scheme for French civil servants — appointed Russell to manage €4.2 billion of foreign-exchange hedging requirements, adding to more than €50 billion of currency exposure Russell already managed as of February 2025. Hawaii's Employees' Retirement System runs two Parametric overlay mandates — $145.5 million in public equity and $186.7 million in public credit, both incepted July 1, 2024 — alongside its core manager lineup. And Russell's transition-management business, which sits adjacent to overlay, managed 99 events and transitioned $88.7 billion in 2024 with a team of 20 implementation professionals. The overlay market is not a niche. It is the operating system for institutional portfolios that have too many managers to coordinate by hand.

The counterargument is that the timeline is the problem, not the solution. A committee that votes in June and does not see a live overlay until October has spent a quarter with its cash idle, its allocation drifting, and its currency unhedged — the exact problems the overlay was hired to fix. The critics have a point, and the industry's answer is to start the paperwork before the mandate is signed: open the accounts, complete the derivatives documentation, and establish the custodian feeds during the selection process, so that the day the contract is signed is the day the trading starts rather than the day the paperwork begins. Causey's advice is the same: get the paperwork and accounts in place because you never know when you will need to start running. The overlay program that is ready before it is needed is the one that earns its fee.

For an allocator, the practical conclusion is to budget three to six months for a full program and four to eight weeks for a currency overlay alone, to start the legal and custodian work during the manager selection rather than after it, and to measure the program on the savings it delivers — the basis points of execution cost, the tracking error it removes, the cash it equitizes — rather than on the speed of its launch. For a provider, it is to be honest about which part of the timeline it controls: the trading can be live in weeks, but the paperwork belongs to the client's lawyers and custodians, and no provider can compress it. And for anyone in the business of overlay data, the missing number is now obvious. Nobody publishes a benchmark for overlay implementation timelines, costs, or savings — the figures in this article come from ADV filings, mandate announcements, and a single transaction-cost analysis. The firm that builds that benchmark will own the next argument in institutional investing.
