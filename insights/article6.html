What Does an Overlay Program Cost an Institutional Portfolio?

An overlay program’s invoice has four lines — management fee, transaction costs, hedging carry, and the staff to run it. Priced from public figures, by mandate type and portfolio size, against the tax savings and risk reduction it is supposed to buy.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

The question came at the end of a long investment committee meeting, when the chief financial officer of a mid-sized public pension fund looked up from the fee schedule and asked the simplest question in the room: what does the overlay actually cost us? The consultant had one number. The custodian’s report had another. The overlay manager’s own invoice had a third, and none of the three agreed — not because anyone was hiding anything, but because an overlay program has no single price. It has four, and they land on four different lines of the budget.

That confusion is the normal state of the market. Overlay management is a small, concentrated business — a handful of specialists, a few large asset managers with implementation arms, and a long tail of consultants who arrange it — and almost nobody publishes a price list. The fees that do surface come from manager searches, regulatory filings, and the occasional research note, and they disagree with each other by factors of two or three. This article prices the program from the figures that are public, mandate by mandate, and then sets the total against what the overlay is supposed to buy: tax savings, volatility reduction, and a portfolio that actually behaves the way the investment policy says it should.

What the mandates cost

Start with the cheapest mandate in the business. A passive currency hedge — a rules-based program that rolls foreign-exchange forwards to hold a fixed hedge ratio, usually 50% or 100% of the foreign exposure — is, by the measure of the specialists who run it, nearly free. Mesirow Currency, one of the largest dedicated currency overlay managers, found in a study of US-dollar funds over 2011–2023 that a specialist manager would typically charge around 1 to 2 basis points to implement a passive hedge. The funds in the same study charged more for the same job done badly: hedged share classes carried an average explicit fee of 4 to 5 basis points, and their total hedging cost — explicit fees plus execution and roll frictions — left them trailing an independently managed passive overlay by about 19 basis points a year on average between 2019 and 2023, underperforming roughly 70% of the time. The lesson is not that hedging is expensive. It is that the cheapest mandate in the business is also the one most often overpaid for, because the buyer cannot see the execution cost inside a fund’s expense ratio.

Add discretion and the price jumps by an order of magnitude. An active currency overlay — a manager who varies the hedge ratio on a view, runs cross hedges, and targets excess return on top of risk reduction — has historically been priced at 15 to 25 basis points, according to Investment & Pensions Europe’s reporting on the market, a range that has survived two decades of fee compression in asset management largely intact. The dispersion is the story: bfinance, the mandate search firm, found in a 2017 study that the most expensive active currency offerings cost twice the cheapest, and that transaction costs across shortlisted managers ranged from 0.46 to 2.68 basis points in a single UK pension fund search — with average trading costs across the market running from under half a basis point to more than 10. A buyer who compares headline fees without comparing transaction costs is comparing the wrong number.

What the active fee buys, when it works, is a return stream that most portfolios cannot get anywhere else. Toby Goodworth of bfinance, speaking at the Global Investment Institute’s Family Office Investment Forum in March 2026, put the benchmarks plainly: a typical active overlay across developed markets runs about 2% a year in tracking error, and a good diversified program should earn an information ratio of about 0.5 — roughly 70 to 100 basis points of excess return a year, uncorrelated with the rest of the portfolio. That is the arithmetic that justifies 15 to 25 basis points. It is also arithmetic that depends entirely on the information ratio holding, and information ratios in currency have a habit of not holding.

The tax overlay is priced differently, because it is sold differently — as a percentage of the assets it manages rather than the exposure it hedges. Direct indexing, the retail version of the tax overlay, now runs at 0.20% to 0.40% a year at Schwab, Fidelity, BlackRock, and Vanguard, with minimums down to $5,000 from $1 million-plus a generation ago. The institutional version, run inside a separately managed account across multiple managers, costs more, and the fee stacks: a model fee, an SMA fee, the advisor’s fee, and the overlay platform charge. Cerulli’s 2026 managed-accounts report puts the category past $1 trillion in assets. The payoff it is measured against is tax alpha — industry estimates cluster at 0.5% to 2.0% a year, consistent with the CFA Institute’s Financial Analysts Journal study of systematic tax-loss harvesting — and Range, a wealth management platform, reported that in 2025 its client accounts using direct indexing harvested an average of $18,281 in losses, versus $4,808 for otherwise similar ETF-only accounts. A tax overlay that costs 30 basis points and delivers 100 basis points of tax alpha is a bargain. One that costs 30 and delivers 20 is a leak.

The completion overlay — the mandate that fills allocation gaps, manages cash, and keeps a multi-manager portfolio at its policy weights — is usually the smallest line on the invoice and the hardest to price, because it is often bundled into an OCIO or implementation mandate. Russell Investments, whose implementation arm reported $81.1 billion in regulatory assets under management at the end of 2025, charges currency mandates as an agreed number of basis points times the mandate size, and warns in its own fee-transparency writing that one provider’s headline fee may include everything while another’s covers only part of the job, with transition management, overlays, and risk management billed piecemeal through the year. The completion overlay is where that warning bites hardest: the fee looks small until the rebalancing trades, the futures rolls, and the cash management charges arrive separately.

The lines nobody budgets

Then there is the line that never appears on any invoice at all: the carry cost of the hedge itself. Hedging a foreign currency means paying the interest-rate differential between the two currencies, and that differential is a real cost. With the Federal Reserve holding the funds rate at 3.75% to 4.00% and the European Central Bank’s deposit rate at 2.50% in late 2026, a European investor hedging dollars pays roughly 1.25 to 1.50 percentage points a year for the privilege — more than fifty times the 2-basis-point management fee on the same hedge. The forward market prices that differential in automatically; the investor pays it whether or not anyone discloses it. In the other direction the carry is a gain, which is why American investors hedging euros in 2026 are being paid to hedge. Carry, not the manager’s fee, is the dominant cost of currency hedging, and it reverses sign with the rate cycle.

Put the lines together for a $1 billion portfolio with 40% — $400 million — in foreign assets, and the shape of the bill becomes clear. A passive currency hedge at 2 basis points on the hedged notional costs $80,000 a year. An active overlay at 20 basis points costs $800,000. A tax overlay at 0.30% on a $200 million taxable sleeve costs $600,000, against tax alpha of 0.5% to 2.0% — $1 million to $4 million a year if the estimates hold, which they do not always do. A $100 million portfolio pays the same rates on a tenth of the assets, but the fixed costs — the custodian’s overlay reporting, the consultant’s review, the legal work on the limited power of attorney — do not shrink by a tenth, which is why smaller institutions so often buy the overlay inside an OCIO mandate and never see the line items at all. These are worked figures from the cited rates, not quotes; any real mandate prices off its own size, complexity, and negotiation.

An overlay program is cheap where it is mechanical, expensive where it is discretionary, and most expensive where its costs are invisible — and the invisible lines are usually the largest.

The honest counterargument

The case against paying any of this is straightforward, and it starts with the arithmetic above. A passive hedge that costs 2 basis points is a bargain; an active overlay that costs 20 and delivers an information ratio of 0.2 instead of 0.5 has cost the portfolio 20 basis points to lose money more cleverly. The tax overlay’s value decays — every loss harvested lowers the cost basis, and by year five in a rising market most accounts have captured the large majority of the lifetime benefit they will ever produce, while the fee recurs every year. And the cheapest argument of all is the one the data supports most often: for a portfolio whose foreign exposure is small, whose tax situation is simple, and whose managers already hedge at the fund level, the overlay adds a layer of fees and governance to solve a problem the portfolio barely has.

None of that makes the overlay a bad purchase. It makes it a purchase whose value is concentrated in specific portfolios: the taxable account with large embedded gains, the pension with a 40% foreign allocation and a board that measures risk in tracking error, the multi-manager structure where two managers are quietly holding opposite sides of the same trade. The fee is small next to those problems. It is large next to nothing.

What to watch

Three questions decide whether the invoice is fair. First, is the fee charged on the assets managed or the exposure hedged — the two differ by a factor of two or more on a 50% hedge ratio, and the distinction is where the most common overcharge hides. Second, are transaction costs disclosed separately, manager by manager, in basis points — bfinance’s 0.46-to-2.68 range in a single search is the reason to ask. Third, does the buyer know the carry cost of every hedge, in both directions, before the mandate starts, rather than discovering it in the performance attribution a year later? An overlay program that can answer all three is probably worth its fee. One that cannot is not cheap at any price.

Overlay management is one of the few services in asset management where the buyer can know the price, the payoff, and the point at which the payoff stops covering the price — if the buyer asks for the four lines separately instead of accepting a single number. The institutions that get value from overlays are not the ones that pay the least. They are the ones that know exactly what each line buys.
