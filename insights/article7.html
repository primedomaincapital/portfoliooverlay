Currency Overlay vs. Interest Rate Overlay: Which Risk Do Institutions Hedge First?

Currency risk moves fast and reprices a portfolio in weeks; interest-rate risk moves slowly and then breaks a pension scheme in a week. The sequencing experienced overlay programs follow — and what 2022 taught about getting it wrong.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In the last week of September 2022, the chief investment officer of a British pension scheme watched three hedges fail in three different ways at the same time. The currency hedge was fine — sterling was collapsing, and the hedge was paying. The interest-rate hedge was the problem: thirty-year gilt yields had jumped 140 basis points in three days, the leveraged liability-driven funds were calling for collateral the scheme did not have, and the Bank of England was about to spend £65 billion buying the very bonds the scheme was being forced to sell. The equity hedge, if it could be called one, was simply the knowledge that the S&P 500 was down 18% for the year and the bond portfolio down 13% alongside it. Three risks, three hedges, three different answers — and the question the board asked afterward was the one every institution with more than one exposure eventually asks: which risk do you hedge first?

The answer depends on which risk can kill the portfolio fastest, which costs the least to remove, and which one the institution is actually exposed to rather than the one it worries about. Currency moves daily and reprices a portfolio in weeks. Interest rates move slowly for years and then break a leveraged structure in a week. Equity beta falls hardest and recovers on its own schedule, which is nobody’s schedule. The sequencing that experienced overlay programs follow is not a ranking of importance. It is a ranking of reversibility: hedge first the risk you cannot afford to be wrong about, then the risk you cannot afford to carry unhedged, and only then the risk you can afford to ride out.

Currency: the fast risk, the cheap hedge

Currency is the risk institutions feel first, because it moves first. The foreign-exchange market turns over about $9.5 trillion a day, according to the Bank for International Settlements’ Triennial Central Bank Survey of April 2025 — up 27% from $7.5 trillion in 2022 — with the dollar on one side of roughly 89% of all trades and London booking 37.8% of the volume. That depth is what makes the currency hedge cheap: the most liquid market in the world, a plain forward contract, a trade that can be put on in an afternoon and taken off the same way.

2022 showed what the unhedged version costs. The dollar index climbed from about 96 at the start of the year to a twenty-year high of 114.78 on September 27 — up roughly 19% year to date — before retreating to 103.52 by year-end, still up about 8% for the year. The euro fell to $0.9535 in September, below parity for the first time since 2002. The yen fell about 14% over the year, from 115.08 to 131.11 per dollar, and Japan’s Ministry of Finance spent about ¥9.18 trillion — roughly $62 billion — intervening in September and October, its first yen-buying intervention since 1998. The engine was the Federal Reserve, which raised rates 425 basis points in 2022 alone on its way to 525 basis points of tightening between March 2022 and July 2023. For a dollar-based investor holding European or Japanese assets, the currency move that year was larger than the equity move in the underlying market — and it arrived in weeks, not years.

The hedge itself is the cheapest in the overlay business. Mesirow Currency puts a passive hedge from a specialist manager at around 1 to 2 basis points. The real cost is the carry: with the Federal Reserve holding the funds rate at 3.75% to 4.00% and the European Central Bank’s deposit rate at 2.50% in late 2026, a European investor hedging dollars pays roughly 1.25 to 1.50 percentage points a year, while an American investor hedging euros is paid to hedge. An active overlay costs more — 15 to 25 basis points, on Investment & Pensions Europe’s reporting — and is judged on a different standard: bfinance’s Toby Goodworth, speaking in March 2026, put a good diversified active program at an information ratio of about 0.5, or 70 to 100 basis points of excess return a year. The complexity is low — forwards, quarterly rolls, no leverage, no collateral spiral — and the hedge is reversible at any time. That combination, fast risk and cheap reversible hedge, is why currency is usually first.

Interest rates: the slow risk, the expensive failure

Interest-rate risk is the opposite: it accumulates quietly for years, and when it breaks, it breaks the balance sheet rather than the quarter. The clearest measure is the funded status of America’s largest corporate pension plans. The Milliman 100 Pension Funding Index put the 100 largest plans at 112.2% funded on August 31, 2026 — $1.299 trillion in assets against $1.158 trillion in liabilities, a $141 billion surplus, and the highest funded ratio since 2001, up from 104.9% a year earlier. The driver was the discount rate, which reached 6.00% in August: when rates rise, the present value of future pension payments falls. The same mechanism runs in reverse — the ratio fell to 112.4% in October from 114.5% in September when the discount rate dropped to 6.20% from 6.47%.

That sensitivity is why pensions hedge rates, and the 2022 gilt crisis is why they now hedge them carefully. Liability-driven investment had grown to roughly £1.5 trillion in notional mandates by 2021, and the pooled corner of the market ran leverage of three to seven times, according to Sarah Breeden, then the Bank of England’s executive director for financial stability. When the thirty-year gilt yield jumped 140 basis points in three days after the September 2022 mini-budget, leveraged funds faced margin calls they could not meet and were forced to sell the very gilts whose fall was causing the calls. The Bank of England committed to buy up to £65 billion of gilts over thirteen days — it ultimately bought about £19 billion — and managers including BlackRock and Mercer spent the following months cutting leverage across their LDI funds. The hedge had worked exactly as designed against the risk it was built for. It had failed against the risk it created: the need for cash, at speed, in a falling market.

The rates hedge is also the one whose payoff is hardest to measure in a single year. BlackRock’s April 2026 analysis estimated that a fully funded plan must now generate 7.3% a year to reach a 110% funded ratio over a decade, up from 4.7% in the low-rate era — a reminder that the same high rates that flatter funded status also raise the bar for everything the plan owns. The allocation shift tells its own story: twenty years ago the Milliman 100 plans held 61.7% of their assets in stocks and 28.6% in bonds; by the end of 2025 that had flipped to 24.1% in stocks and 52.7% in bonds. The rate hedge, for most large plans, is no longer a trade. It is the portfolio.

Equity beta: the risk you hedge last, or never

Equity beta is the risk institutions talk about most and hedge least, for a simple reason: it is the return engine. Hedging beta means paying to remove the thing the portfolio exists to own. In 2022 the S&P 500 returned −18.1% and the Bloomberg US Aggregate Bond Index −13.0% — the worst year for the bond index since 1976, and the first year on record in which both fell more than 10% at the same time. A 60/40 blend lost about 17%. Those numbers look like an argument for hedging beta. They are also the reason the argument usually loses: the hedge that would have protected 2022 — index puts, futures overlays, tail-risk programs — costs carry every year it is not needed, rolls constantly, and gives back a large part of its protection in the recovery, when the market rises fastest and the hedge is still on.

The overlay’s answer to beta is quieter than a hedge and cheaper than one: rebalancing. A completion overlay that keeps a multi-manager portfolio at its policy weights sells what has risen and buys what has fallen, which is the only beta control that pays the institution instead of charging it. It does not prevent a 2022. It prevents the portfolio from arriving at the next one holding twice the equity weight its policy allows because nobody rebalanced for three years. The explicit beta hedge is reserved for the portfolios that cannot afford a drawdown at all: the plan about to annuitize, the endowment about to fund a capital project, the fund with a hard liability date.

Currency is hedged because it is cheap and fast. Rates are hedged because they decide whether the plan survives. Beta is hedged last because it is the reason the portfolio exists.

The honest counterargument

The sequencing above can be wrong in every direction, and 2022 is the evidence. A scheme that hedged rates first and currency second did exactly what the framework recommends — and still nearly failed, because the rates hedge itself was leveraged and the leverage was the risk. A fund that hedged currency first paid 1.5 percentage points a year of carry to protect against a move that, for a long-horizon investor, partly reverses itself: the dollar that peaked at 114.78 in September 2022 was back near 103 by year-end, and the investor who hedged at the peak paid the carry and kept none of the reversal. And the whole debate assumes the institution can hedge at all. A currency forward takes an afternoon; a rates program takes an ISDA agreement, a collateral framework, and a board that understands all three — months of governance before the first trade.

There is also a serious argument that the sequencing question is the wrong question, because the risks are not independent. The 2022 dollar surge and the gilt crisis shared a single cause — the fastest tightening cycle in four decades — and a program that hedged currency and rates as separate problems, on separate timelines, with separate managers, was hedging one shock twice and the interaction between the two shocks not at all. The overlay’s real job is not to rank risks. It is to see them as one portfolio, which is the one thing a collection of single-risk hedges cannot do.

What to watch

The sequencing that holds up, across the portfolios that have run overlays longest, follows the liability rather than the market. A defined-benefit pension with dated liabilities hedges rates first, through LDI, because the liability is the risk — currency second, on the foreign allocation, and beta through rebalancing. A global equity-heavy endowment or foundation with no dated liabilities hedges currency first, because it is cheap, fast, and reversible, and treats rates as a portfolio-construction question rather than a hedge. A taxable multi-manager portfolio hedges tax first, because the tax overlay’s payoff is the largest and the most certain of the three, and currency second. In every case the test is the same: can the institution name the carry cost of each hedge, the collateral it must hold, and the condition under which it would take the hedge off? If it cannot answer all three, it is not running an overlay program. It is collecting hedges.

Hedge the risk that can force you to sell first. That is usually rates, for a pension, and currency, for everyone else. Hedge the risk that can make you look foolish second. And leave beta alone until the portfolio has a date on which it must be whole — because a hedge against the market’s downside is also a hedge against the market’s upside, and the upside is what the portfolio is for.
