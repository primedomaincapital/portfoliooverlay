The Tax Overlay Takes the Wheel: Direct Indexing Passes $1 Trillion

Cerulli’s latest count puts direct indexing past $1 trillion — ahead of its own forecast. Tax management is now the top development priority at 76% of platform sponsors. The overlay business has a new center of gravity.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

In a platform sponsor’s 2026 planning meeting, the top development priority was not a new model portfolio, a new risk tool, or a new manager lineup. For the second year running, “improving tax management capabilities” ranked first, cited by 76% of firms in Cerulli’s U.S. Managed Accounts 2026 report. Tax-loss harvesting has become close to standard, with 79% of sponsor firms reporting an automated tool in place, up from 72% in 2025. The room was not debating stock picks. It was debating taxes.

The growth behind that priority is now measured in trillions. Direct indexing has become the first retail separately managed account strategy to top $1 trillion in assets, according to Cerulli’s 2026 managed-accounts report — ahead of the firm’s own earlier projection of roughly $825 billion by the end of 2026, a 12.3% annual growth rate from about $462 billion at the end of 2022. The category outran its own forecast. Separately managed accounts grew total retail assets 22.5% over the year to $4.67 trillion, net flows to managed account programs topped $1 trillion for the first time in 2025 at $1.08 trillion, and Cerulli projects total managed account assets will exceed $20 trillion by 2027 and $25 trillion by 2029. Financial Advisor’s report on the Cerulli study carries the $1 trillion milestone and the flow figures; Trading Market Signals’ June 2026 analysis records the earlier $825 billion projection and the 12.3% growth rate it has now beaten.

The reason is access, not marketing. Minimums that once required $1 million or more at a private bank have fallen to $5,000 to $100,000 at Schwab, Fidelity, BlackRock, and Vanguard, with annual fees of 0.20% to 0.40%. Tax-managed SMA assets passed $500 billion in 2024, up 67% from 2022. The tax benefit is measurable: Range, a wealth management platform, reported that in 2025 its client accounts using direct indexing harvested an average of $18,281 in losses, versus $4,808 for otherwise similar ETF-only accounts — nearly a fourfold difference. Industry estimates of the resulting tax alpha commonly cluster in the 0.5% to 2.0% annual range, consistent with the CFA Institute’s Financial Analysts Journal study of systematic tax-loss harvesting. MutualFunds.com’s review of the shift documents the minimums, the fees, and the harvesting gap.

The plumbing is what makes the overlay matter. A direct-indexed account holds the individual stocks of an index inside an SMA wrapper, so only the SMA harvests losses at the single-stock level — the source of tax alpha a packaged fund structurally cannot deliver. The limits are just as mechanical. Harvested losses can offset only $3,000 of ordinary income a year under IRC Section 1211(b), a cap unadjusted for inflation since 1978, with the excess carried forward. An investor in the 0% long-term capital gains bracket — taxable income up to roughly $49,450 for single filers and $98,900 for joint filers in 2026 — has little to gain from harvesting in the first place. And 62% of wealth management professionals globally expect to increasingly use direct indexing in the next three years, according to MSCI’s 2026 Wealth Trends report, which means the capacity question is now a competitive one.

The overlay stopped being a risk tool with a tax feature. It is now a tax tool with a risk feature — and the firms that still sell it the old way are selling last decade’s product.

The honest counterargument

Tax alpha decays, and the decay is built into the design. Every loss harvested lowers that lot’s cost basis, shrinking the pool of future losses available; by year five in a generally rising market, most accounts have captured the large majority of the lifetime tax benefit they will ever produce. Fee stacking is the quiet leak: a model-portfolio fee, an SMA management fee, the advisor’s own fee, and an overlay platform charge can compound into a number that erodes the very tax alpha the structure was built to capture. And the competition has arrived from an unexpected direction — tax-aware long/short strategies, a category Cerulli began tracking only in the fourth quarter of 2025, already rank as the seventh-largest SMA asset class at $116.5 billion.

None of that makes the tax overlay a bad product. It makes it a product whose value is front-loaded, fee-sensitive, and account-specific — the opposite of the permanent, portfolio-wide benefit the marketing implies. Only 47% of advisors currently integrate SMAs into client accounts, and 29% of sponsors have automated asset-location optimization, so the industry is still early in delivering the coordinated, household-level tax management that Cerulli names as sponsors’ top technology priority.

What to watch

Unified managed household platforms — which coordinate tax treatment across a client’s full set of accounts rather than one account at a time — are the next test. If they work, the overlay’s value extends beyond the single account and the basis-depletion problem matters less. If they do not, direct indexing’s growth will slow as the early-adopter cohort hits year five and the harvested-loss pool runs dry. Either way, overlay selection is now a tax-engineering decision, and the buyer should ask for after-tax numbers by account, not composite returns.

Portfolio overlay programs were built to control risk and harvest tax losses. In 2026, the market has voted on which of those two jobs matters more — and the vote was not close.
