Tax Overlay vs. Completion Overlay: Strategies Explained

Tax overlay harvests losses across managers. Completion overlay fills allocation gaps. Here's how the two core overlay strategies differ — and when to use each.

Prime Domain Capital · Market Intelligence

Ask three CIOs what "overlay" means and you may get three answers. One will talk about tax-loss harvesting. Another will describe filling the gaps between managers. A third will mention currency hedging. They are all describing overlay management — but they are describing different strategies that happen to share a name and an operational chassis.

The two foundational strategies are tax overlay and completion overlay. They solve different problems, use different tools, and appeal to different clients. Understanding the distinction is the first step to knowing which one — or whether both — your portfolio needs.

Tax Overlay: Keeping More of What You Earn

Tax overlay is the more widely adopted of the two, and the math behind it is the more compelling. The premise: in a multi-manager taxable portfolio, each manager trades to maximize pre-tax return within its mandate. None of them is managing the portfolio's tax bill. The result is predictable — gains realized unnecessarily, losses left unharvested, wash-sale violations across managers, and a tax drag that compounds silently year after year.

A tax overlay manager takes control of the implementation layer. The underlying managers still make the investment decisions — they submit their target trades. But the overlay desk decides how, when, and whether to execute them, optimizing across the full portfolio for after-tax outcomes.

The toolkit is specific. Loss harvesting: systematically realizing losses in positions that have declined, while maintaining the portfolio's economic exposure through correlated substitutes. If the growth manager's Apple position is down 12%, the overlay desk can sell it, book the loss, and buy a correlated large-cap tech exposure — staying invested while banking a tax asset. Gain deferral: when a manager wants to sell a winner, the overlay desk evaluates whether the sale can wait until the gain qualifies for long-term treatment, or whether it can be paired against harvested losses elsewhere. Wash-sale management: tracking the 30-day windows across all managers so that a loss harvested in one sleeve isn't disallowed because another manager bought the same security.

The results are well documented. Parametric's long-running research on tax-managed core equity suggests that systematic tax-loss harvesting adds roughly 1 to 2 percent annually in after-tax excess return over a full market cycle, with the benefit concentrated in volatile markets (more losses to harvest) and high-tax-bracket clients (more value per dollar of loss). For a taxable family office portfolio of $200 million, that is $2 to $4 million a year in tax alpha — against an overlay fee of perhaps $200,000 to $400,000.

"Taxes are the largest expense in most taxable portfolios — larger than fees, larger than trading costs. Yet most multi-manager portfolios manage every expense except the biggest one."

Completion Overlay: Filling the Gaps

Completion overlay solves a different problem: the portfolio you have is not the portfolio you targeted. This happens constantly. A small-cap manager drifts into mid-caps. An international manager's country bets wander. Cash accumulates between manager funding cycles. A new allocation to private credit is committed but not yet called, leaving the portfolio underweight its target.

Rather than firing managers or constantly rebalancing through expensive physical trades, the completion overlay uses derivatives — futures, swaps, ETFs — to bring the portfolio back to target efficiently. If the policy allocation calls for 8% emerging markets but the current managers only sum to 6.5%, the overlay desk buys emerging-market futures to close the gap. If a manager's style has drifted, the overlay can short the drifted exposure and buy the intended one.

The classic use case is the portable alpha structure, though the completion overlay is broader than that. More commonly today, it is used for cash equitization (keeping uninvested cash fully exposed to the market via futures rather than sitting in T-bills), rebalancing (maintaining allocation bands without disrupting managers), and transition completion (holding the target allocation steady during manager changes).

Completion overlay is also the tool for what consultants call "completion portfolios" — a dedicated sleeve, often 5 to 15% of the fund, managed by the overlay provider to offset the aggregate active bets of the underlying managers and pull the total portfolio back toward the policy benchmark. Russell Investments pioneered this approach for pension clients in the 2000s, and it remains a staple of the institutional overlay market.

Which Do You Need?

The honest answer for most large taxable multi-manager portfolios is both — and most overlay providers offer them as a combined mandate. The tax overlay runs continuously on the equity sleeves; the completion overlay manages allocation, cash, and drift across the whole fund.

But the priority depends on the portfolio. Taxable family offices and high-net-worth multi-manager accounts should start with tax overlay; the after-tax benefit dwarfs everything else. Tax-exempt pensions and endowments get no benefit from tax management, so completion overlay — rebalancing, cash equitization, drift control — is the relevant service. Foundations with both taxable and tax-exempt pools need the overlay manager to keep the two strictly separated, since harvesting losses in the wrong pool is worse than useless.

The deeper point: overlay is not one product. It is an operational capability — aggregated portfolio visibility, centralized trade implementation, continuous optimization — applied to whichever frictions cost the portfolio most. Tax and completion are the two classic applications. Currency overlay, ESG overlay, and liability-driven overlay are the newer ones. The chassis is the same. The strategy depends on what is leaking.
